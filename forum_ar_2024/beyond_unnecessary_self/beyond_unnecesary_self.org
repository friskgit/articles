* Abstract
** Beyond the unnecessary self

In my 2013 paper /The (un)necessary self/ [cite:@Frisk2013] I explored the idea of the /giving up of the self/ as an important step towards the dismantling of the romantic idea of creation, and approach an understanding of creativity that is more closely aligned with the Other. The other should be understood as anyone in the proximity of the artistic practice: a co-creator, a listener, a particpant or a remote collaborator. The point here is to move the focus from the creator to what is created and to understand the roles of the various agents involved.

In music, hyper-capitalism of the twenty first century is eager to commodify the artistic output, the artist, as well as the listeners. This is not only a problem for the freedom of art (a concept equally complex), it also makes the role of the self difficult to understand. But in the radicalization of the role of the creator, both a new work concept and a review of the self is necessary, even beyond the notion of giving up of the self. The ethics in artistic practices, that is, the moral values that are expressed through artistic practices in music, specifically improvisation, may complement traditional views on ethics and is an important aspect when discussing the roles of the self. The notion of the /Care of the Self/, as discussed in Michel Foucault's Volume Three of the /History of Sexuality/, is used as a method to approach this complex area. 

# This presentation will be in the form of a lecture performance.
* Beyond the unnecessary self
** Introduction

In my 2013 paper /The (un)necessary self/ [cite:@Frisk2013] I explored the idea of the concept of /giving up of the self/ as an important step towards the dismantling of the romantic idea of creation and individuality.
I wanted to approach an understanding of creativity that is entangled with the other in a way that emphasizes /relations/ rather than the individuality of the artist.
Related to this is the ambition to move the focus from the /creator/ to what is /created/, and to better understand the roles of the various actors involved.
The /other/ in this context should be understood as anyone or anything in the proximity of the artistic practice: a co-creator, a listener, a particpant, an instrument, a remote collaborator, or anything with similar impact on the artistic practice.
This attitude has some resemblance to the flat ontology and the theories promoted by the French sociologist and philosopher Bruno Latour, such as actor-network theory, but also depart from them in some important ways, primarily the essentialist view on certain of the aspects included here.
At a later stage it would be interesting to make an ANT oriented analysis of the material I am discussing here.

An aspect that I did not explore in my earlier paper, which I refer to above, was that of the various transactions that take place in artistic production.
The hyper-capitalism of the twenty-first century is eager to commodify any artistic output, as well as the artist and the listeners, as objects for consumption.
Though this may not at first appear to have a big impact on experimental art practices my claim here is that it still influences some key factors that contribute to how artistic practices in music may develop and, more importantly, how the self develops.
One crucial component is the opaque nature of the relations between commodified objects in commercial media. 
For example, the primary interest of the big media streaming platforms is not necessarily to deliver music or movies.
Instead, it is the eternal collection of data that happens in the background that represents real value in this system.

This is not a new phenomenon.
The main point of commercial media has always been to sell space for advertisement, and the role of the content is to draw attention to the right demographic groups--those to whom the commercials are directed.
Capitalism promotes itself based on the freedom it says it allows for to the individual to whom the liberty to choose and consume at free will is given.
However, this freedom is debatable when the main economic transaction happens hidden from the user and sometimes takes place in completely different domains.
Furthermore, even the concept of freedom itself is a complex area that should be carefully approached, and the way the freedom of capitalism and the freedom of artistic practice diverge from each other may easily create conceptual confusion.
Finally, the connection between freedom and ethics contributes to how the self is situated, or rather relocated, in this complex matter.[fn:2]

Against this backdrop I will attempt to describe how a practice of improvisation with obsolete electronic instruments can use a different conception of freedom to deconstruct both the self and the powers of commodification.
Exploring the ethics of an artistic practice is a necessary part of this process.
Both here, and in my original essay from 2013 cited above, American writer David Henry Thoreau's famous notion of the absent speaker has served as an inspiration: "The peculiarity of a work of genius is the absence of the speaker from his speech" [cite:@thoreau2004; p. 264].
Leaving the notion of genius to the side, this /absent speaker/ could be seen to be related to the unnecessary self: only the aspects of the self necessary for the performance needs to be present, the rest can become a distraction.
The partly disruptive dynamics of hyper capitalism with its focus on self realization through consumption which is briefly discussed above plays an important role here.

** Playing obsolete instruments
In my engagement to develop a historically informed performance practice in electronic music I find that the role of the self comes to the forefront in new and interesting ways. 
Previous possibly hidden features of these historic instruments, explored through an artistic practice in which the self's relation to the self is explored, may now be discerned and re-contextualized, something I will return to later.
The giving up of the self is not to succumb to someone else but depends on the willingness to engage in a dialogue with /others/, which also includes instruments and other non-human things, concerning the creative process.
The instruments, and the practice to play them, that I will be discussing here, here are a modular synthesizer and audio mixer designed in Sweden in the early the 1970's (See Fig ref:fig:dataton_1).
When playing these modules the /other/ may be defined as the greater context of these instruments, some of which are unknown, others will reveal themself through the practice.
In a slow and meticulus process of untangling the ways in which these instruments can be approached (see [cite:@holzer2021,;@Frisk2025;@Frisk2021a]) a certain proficiency with the interface is developed, always in interaction with the objects themselves.
In other words, rather than developing a compositional or improvisatory framework that I try to superimpose on the instruments, I discover the possibilities in interaction with the greater context of them.
Thus, to attempt to understand the qualities and the particularity of the /Dataton 3000/ modules, further discussed below, a wide range of parameters need to be considered.
Should this analysis not be successful there is a risk that the instrument's proper qualities are misunderstood, or that one ends up recreating what has already been done with it, or both.
It is, however, the relations between these attributes that I am interested in, not my dominance on one, or one parameter's dominance over me.

In a wider scope of contemporary technologies, the rate at which the development in general is progressing can turn objects sometimes less than a decade old incompatible with current systems, which makes working with these modules even more interesting.
What are the properties, or uses, of these instruments that have been concealed by the quick development of contemporary music technology?
An experimental hypothesis is that by learning from these early instruments it may be possible to better understand the complexities of improvisatory practices in electronic music.

In an artistic practice that departs from use of obsolete instruments the interrelations between the various subjects and objects involved in the performance can become complicated, but they may also disclose the challenges involved in a fruitful manner.
The most prominent is the aspect of technology itself.
Though many examples of inventive designs are incorporated into mainstream products, others are simply forgotten and abandoned despite their sometimes explicit contribution to the development.
The race for inventing new and even more laborious electronic instruments and interfaces, fueled both by inventiveness and need and by market economy and capitalism, makes artistic practices involving these instruments fragile and vulnerable to changes that renders all, or parts of the instruments, unusable.
This may be software upgrades, abandoned hardware circuits, forgotten system-specific knowledge, or simply the fact that the new upgrade is so much more compelling in its usability and sonic or musical qualities that it renders the old instruments obsolete.
The violin and the piano are generally not upgraded in this fashion so this is a particular trait of electronic instruments.

That commercial aspects have a great impact in this field is also validated by Pinch and Trocco when they conclude that "from a flexible variety of possible control configurations, the synthesizer eventually stabilised into a keyboard instrument" [cite:@Pinch1998; 28].
In other words, over time the highly experimental field of design of electronic musical instruments is narrowed, and standardization in collaboration with market forces exclude those items that are not successful enough, or simply incompatible with current production paradigms.
This brings us back to the discussion relating to the impact that capitalism has on the field.
The instruments that contributed to an otherwise experimental artistic practice that may have tried to evade capitalistic market forces are nevertheless sucked into to the commanding  efficiency and normalization of production and design expected from the market forces.
Repudiated and eventually incompatible with supporting systems such developed interfaces may eventually be rendered useless by the market.
It is true that no Western instruments, electronic or acoustic, are independent from the commodifying forces or market economy, but the way that the development of electronic instrument is aligned with the advancements of technology in society in general makes them particularly sensitive to this process.
A certain merging of the fields is also occurring here where tools of communication such as the mobile phone are also used and explored for artistic performances, again bringing the complexities of compatibilities to the fore (see [cite/text:@Wang2014;@Madhavan2016]).

There are both differences and similarities between the study of traditional methods for musical interpretation, rooted in Western musical history and practices, and the excavation of the technological and cultural significance of a particular electronic musical instrument of the past.
Since at least the romantic era the definition of the artistic work has been constructed from the vantage point of the composer in a strictly hierarchical structure with the self of the originator at the centre.
This is well described by the French literary critic and philosopher Roland Barthes in his famous essay /The death of the Author/ [cite:@barthes68:death_of], where the basic critique is that detailed biographical, geographical and historical knowledge about the composer, rather than the reader and the tet itself, is at the core of all traditional interpretations of a work.
Hence, following this line of thought a true understanding of the work has to go through the understanding of a self something which is made complicated by the amalgamation of the self with the work.
In this structural organization of musical work not only the music as a product is susceptible to commodification, but also, or perhaps even more so, is the author the one being commodified.
My distancing, or giving up of, myself is part of my attempt to distance myself from this view and instead establish a artistic practice that where the self is in constant motion, where there is no center, only relations. The work concept in this case will also be in constant motion rendering a structural analysis rather meaningsless.

As already mentioned the instruments in this study through which I have explored the processes of the self are the /Dataton System 3000/ [cite:@sandlund2019].
This modular synthesizer and audio mixer was designed by Björn Sandlund in Sweden during the 1970’s in an attempt to make a versatile instrument for electronic music and pedagogic uses.
Despite its utopian vision The /Dataton System 3000/ was never widely used as a musical instrument, nor was it explored for its pedagogical enterprise.
It thus exemplifies the kind of harsh expulsion that the market carries out on unwanted and useless objects.
My primary goal here is not the objects in themselves, nor is it grounded in a fetischism for pre-digital equipment.
My interest in these instruments lies in the attempt to understand them in their wider context and map this knowledge unto my musical interests and experimentation.
This includes but is not limited to their historical origins.
One could argue that there is nothing outside a piece of equipment like a Dataton module, to rudily paraphrase Derrida's statement that /il n’y a pas de hors-texte/ [cite:@derrida2016].
That there is only the object and what it tells us in the context that it is used in and where the /reader/ decides what they are, which is essentially Roland Barthes point as discussed above.
Everything is there, their narrative is contained in the objects.
Though I believe that this line of thinking is useful there is still a risk of creating hierarchies in the relations between the various actors if the instruments and the reader are put at the centre in this way.
Neither is it my interest to design an artistically driven project with the end goal is a /work/, which undoubtedly also would construct a hierarchy around the artifact.

Rather, it is the interactions that are made possible by engaging in an artistically driven play with the objects that I am after.
A free play that does not have a purpose and no particular meaning, though it may well develop in other directions in.
The result may be one or several works, but they are not the goal, they are subordinate to the process.
In this free play I can engage with certain questions in a way that would otherwise not have been possible, and improvisation is part of the method that allows me to do this.

Narrowing this down to the field of music there are, crudely put, two models of analysis that emerge, the traditional musicological and historical approach, and a historically informed exploration of a electronic music heritage.
The first anchors the work to a composer with the work at its origin, and the second attempts to support a network of relations that also includes an originator but does not necessarily privilege them.
The biggest difference between the two models of analysis is that the first, in most cases anchors the composer at this origin, and the second attempts to support a network of relations that also includes an originator but does not necessarily privilege them.
The ladder, I would argue, is not normative on the level of practice, it doesn't prescribe how one should do something, but it rather proposes how one may understand something one already does.
The practice analyzed here, improvisation, is a method or a form that freedom can take.
In this freedom a different view on the self can emerge.
The added value is that this model presents is an expanded ethical dimension that is likewise relived by the freedom in artistic practice.

** Freedom
The freedom of art gives the role of the /self/ many different faces and not all of them are necessarily practical in the artistic practice.
Returning to Thoreau he makes an attempt at releasing the self of the author from the work through a process of self-effacement where the individual ego or personality of the author is dissolved.
This is made extremely difficult at a time where the works are exploited on a market where the transactions are so convoluted and opaque that they are impossible to disentangle?
In the attempt to understand the role of the creator, the self, both a new work concept and a review of the self is necessary.
In the way that these relations may be unwrapped the artistic practice may be seen to have a particular form for ethics, which allows for another analysis of the self.
The ethics in artistic practices, that is, the moral values that are expressed through artistic practices in music, specifically improvisation, is by all means an important aspect when investigating the roles of the self.
These values are disclosed in the way music is negotiated in improvisation and may turn out to work in ways that are different, or even opposed to social ethics.

The notion of the /Care of the Self/, as discussed in Michel Foucault's Volume Three of the /History of Sexuality/ [cite:@foucault1988], is used here as a method to approach this complex area. 
The /care of the self/ is a particular development between self and others, a method for developing an ethics through engaging with the self's relation to the self, a self that is rooted in "practices of freedom" [cite:@Foucault1997-2; 283]: "Freedom is the ontological condition of ethics. But ethics is the considered form that freedom takes when it is informed by reflection" [cite:@Foucault1997-2; 284]. The care of the self is the activity of which these elements are a part, and it is not a solipsitic activity that is merely concerned with the self:

#+begin_quote
When you take care of the body you do not take care of the self. The self is not clothing, tools, or possessions; it is to be found in the principle that uses these tools, a principle not of the body but of the soul. You have to worry about your soul--that is the principal activity of caring for yourself. The care of the self is the care of the activity and not the care of the soul-as-substance. [cite:@Foucault1997-2; 230f]
#+end_quote

Translated to artistic practice the last sentence of this quotation may be reformulated: The care of the self is the care of the artistic activity and not the care of the soul-as-substance.
The artistic practice, the doing, transcends the self and has priority over it.
The meaning of soul-as-substance is similar to Foucault's famous quote that “The soul is the prison of the body” [cite:@foucault1977; 31] and relates to the soul as a set of techniques for domination.
Hence, I argue that there is a useful relation here between the concept of the soul-as-substance--as well as the general critique of the author--and the ambition to give up the self in artistic practices.
It may even be necessary to give up the self in order to see the practice as an instrument of freedom.
Perhaps it is counter-intuitive that giving up the self is a similar process to the care for the self.
In my reading and my understanding of the care for the self the activity, or the practice, is a condition for the care for the self that is allowed for only if the self, or soul-as-substance, does not interfere and reduce the freedom.
The soul-as-substance is a domination of the self through capitalism in ways that makes the subject normative rather than one that allows for an engagement of free play.
It is a threat to freedom as a necessary condition for improvisation.
The principle that uses the tools of artistic practice is in essence the aesthetics of the creative act rather than the self.

Though Thoreau may not have thought of it this way, the absent speaker, that was discussed above, is an odd but interesting reference to the care of the self.
Both gives priority to the activity.
However, the most important one is the notion of the practice as the tool to develop the care for the self.
The /giving up of the self/ and the /care of the self/ are not contradictions - they feed upon each other.
They both allow for an epistemology of creativity, an understanding of practice as the core of human activity that is more closely aligned with others than the self.
The care for the self is that care of the relations.

The expansion of the notion of the unnecessary self was inspired by the line of thoughts presented in the paper /Sounds of Future Past/ [cite:@Frisk2021a] where the concept of an /ethics of instruments/ is explored.
This radical idea was first presented by professor of history of art John Tresch and professor of music Emily I. Dolan in the paper /Toward an Organology of Music/ [cite:@Tresch2013].
It is based on some of the conditions for, or elements of, ethical relations which in turn connects to Foucault's notion of the /technologies of the self/ [cite:@foucault1988b].
Tresch and Dolan explore this experimental ethics of instruments through four analytical categories which in essence are adaptation of the four axes that makes up "Foucault’s analysis of the self’s relation to the self", but they "have tweaked and tuned them in order to apply them to instruments" [cite:@Tresch2013; p. 284]. The four categories relate to the instrument's materiality, its mediation or level of presence, its greater context and its telos[fn:1] (see [cite/text:@Holzer2025] for a more indepth discussion).
Their idea of an ethics of instruments is leaning on the notion that these categories can provide grounds for an analysis of an ethics of instruments.
As a model of analysis it provides a possible framework for analyzing and understanding obsolete instruments and the possible ways in which difficult questions relating to topics such as accuracy, responsibility and freedom may be approached.
Whether or not it is possible to consider that material objects possess a notion of ethics is a different question and beyond the scope of this paper, but one may argue that priority should be given to ensure that all humans and animals enjoy basic ethical living conditions first.

Freedom may be seen as the ontological condition for ethics as well as for improvisation, though this is somewhat dependent on the style of improvisation.
Either way it appears to open up a possible connection between ethics and improvisation in that both depend on some notion of freedom.
This freedom, however, is elusive and may easily be mistaken for the freedom of the self, a freedom rather attached to the idea of the soul-as-substance and self-emancipation, and the freedom of the consumer in a capitalist market economy.
But if improvisation is to be considered an act of ethics a further paraphrasing of the quote above by Foucault may read as: /Musical improvisation is one of the considered forms that freedom takes when it is informed by reflection/.
This assumes that the reflective act, necessary in most artistic processes, is the mediator between freedom and improvisation.
It is the activity that instantiates a recursive process between freedom and improvisation.

As Foucault points out, the care of the self is the care of the activity, and in improvisation it is the artistic practice is what enables this.
The ethics of improvisation, then, arises from freedom.
The condition for these relations to develop is the self's reflection upon itself and upon the practice, but it is mainly the results of this process that are of interest, not the self itself.
This can be compared to philosopher Gary Peters remark that "free-improvisation is not driven by a concern for other improvisers, but by a concern or care for the work itself" [cite:@benson2003; p. 59].
My claim is that improvisation is driven by the process of a care for the self.

** Final notes
My argument here has been that with the tools that are defined through the technologies of the self, the notion of the care of the self opens up a focus on the relations between the self and the things that surround it.
Relations that eventually renders the actual self less important.
Whether or not one is willing to accept the idea of an ethics of instruments is not the main point here, but what can be gathered from Tresch and Dolan is that the self's relation to the self promotes the autonomy of some of these objects.
Through the unwrapping of the various discourses of power, also an the broad level, such as the ones between improvisation and freedom, self and other, and self and self, the particularities and aptitudes of the instruments discussed here, the Dataton 3000, are revealed.
By way of a preliminary understanding of an ethics of instruments these relations exist, matter and they need to be good and respectful.
In this sense ethical relations may appear important between all objects in the network that constitute the context of musical improvisation.
Even if this does not extend all the way to an ethics of instruments, a deep understanding for these relations may contribute to a developed sense of ethics, an ethics of artistic practice in improvisation.
In this space the /care for the self/ is the prcess that deconstructs the object/subject division necessary for this process to unfold  (see [cite/text:@bergamin2023] for a realted discussion on this topic).


In the attempt to construct an artistic practice where the self as subject is unnecessary there are a number of factors that have been discussed here.
One is the objectifying forces of the hyper capitalism that not even experimental musical practices manage to evade.
Creating sustainable relations between the self, the practices of the self, and others is made difficult by the logics of the global market economy.
Another is the influence of the instruments the practice is engaged with and the fact that these themselves may engage in a process of subjectification that has an important role on the general ethics of the practice.
Yet another is that the general ethics of the artistic practice as firmly rooted in the care for the self is constantly challenged by external forces that impact, if not the freedom, at lease the way that freedom is understood.
Nevertheless, through the practice of the care of the self I believe that a genuine and useful idea of an ethics of artistic practice that may resist the influence of capitalism is possible.
By putting focus on the other--the other here should be understood as everything else than the subject--in artistic practice, primarily improvisation, the a practice will be strong enough to render the self unnecessary.
